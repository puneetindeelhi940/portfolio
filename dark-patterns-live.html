Demo 01 · Urgency

The countdown that never ends

“Hurry — your discount expires!” Watch the timer. Now reload the page.

checkout.example-store
⚡ FLASH DEAL — 40% OFF
14:59
Offer expires when the timer hits zero
Reveal · How the trick works

The clock resets every time you arrive.

The timer isn’t counting toward any real deadline — it’s reset on page load and often just loops. It exploits loss aversion and scarcity bias: the fear of missing out overrides deliberate evaluation, so you buy before you’ve decided you want to.

Harm: purchases made under manufactured pressure, buyer’s remorse, eroded trust.

Honest version: no timer, or a real deadline that genuinely passes. Let the product’s value — not a fake clock — do the convincing.

Demo 02 · Misdirection

Confirmshaming: the guilt-trip “no”

Try to decline this newsletter pop-up. Read how the “no” is worded.

example-blog.com

Want to actually get ahead in life?

Join 50,000 winners getting our weekly success secrets.

Reveal · How the trick works

Your decline is written to make you feel bad.

Confirmshaming loads the rejection option with self-criticism (“staying mediocre”). It weaponises ego and social desirability — you don’t want to click something that labels you a failure, so you accept.

Harm: coerced consent, unwanted signups, resentment.

Honest version: a neutral “No thanks” that respects the choice equally to “Yes.”

Demo 03 · Sneaking

Drip pricing: the total that keeps climbing

A $29 ticket. Click through checkout and watch the fees appear one screen at a time.

tickets.example.com
General Admission × 1$29.00
Total$29.00
Reveal · How the trick works

The real price is hidden until you’re committed.

Drip pricing reveals mandatory fees only after you’ve invested time and feel psychologically committed — the sunk-cost fallacy. By the final screen the $29 ticket costs far more, but abandoning “feels” wasteful.

Harm: overpaying, distorted price comparison, a market that rewards hiding costs.

Honest version: show the all-in price up front. Regulators increasingly mandate exactly this.

Demo 04 · Sneaking

Pre-ticked boxes & the snuck-in add-on

This “order summary” has already made some choices for you. Spot them before you’d pay.

shop.example.com/cart
Wireless Headphones$89.00
Shipping Protection (recommended)$6.99
Reveal · How the trick works

Every default was chosen for the seller, not you.

The headphones were $89, but shipping protection was snuck in and three boxes are pre-ticked. This exploits default bias — people overwhelmingly leave defaults untouched. The “recommended” label adds false authority.

Harm: paying for things you never chose, unwanted marketing, over-sharing.

Honest version: nothing added or pre-checked. The user opts in to extras, not out of them.

Demo 05 · Obstruction

Roach motel: easy in, impossible out

Subscribing is one tap. Now try to cancel.

app.example-stream.com

You’re subscribed to Stream+ Premium — $14.99/mo.

Reveal · How the trick works

The exit is a maze; the entrance was a door.

The roach motel creates deliberate asymmetry: signing up is frictionless, cancelling is a gauntlet of confirmations, retention offers, and dead ends. It banks on effort aversion — many people simply give up and keep paying.

Harm: unwanted recurring charges, wasted time, feeling trapped.

Honest version: cancelling is exactly as easy as subscribing — one click, no gauntlet. “Click to cancel” laws now require this.

Demo 06 · Misdirection

Visual interference: where’s the free option?

One button is screaming at you. The choice you probably want is hiding.

example-app.com/upgrade

You’ve hit your free limit. Choose a plan:

continue with limited free plan
Reveal · How the trick works

Contrast steers your click.

Visual interference uses size, colour and placement to make the profitable option unmissable and the free option nearly invisible. Your attention follows visual salience, not your actual intent.

Harm: unnecessary upgrades, decisions made by design hierarchy rather than need.

Honest version: both options get equal visual weight so the choice is genuinely yours.

Demo 07 · Urgency / Scarcity

Fake scarcity & the “live” viewer counter

Only a few left — and look how many people are watching! (Watch the numbers.)

book.example-hotels.com
Seaview Room · $189/night
🔥 Only 2 rooms left at this price!
23 people are viewing this right now
Reveal · How the trick works

The numbers are invented — refresh and they jump.

“Only 2 left” and the live viewer count are frequently randomly generated, not real inventory or traffic. They stack scarcity and social proof to create a herd-driven rush.

Harm: panic booking, overpaying, decisions driven by fabricated competition.

Honest version: show real availability, or nothing. If a number can’t be verified, it shouldn’t pressure a decision.

Demo 08 · Misdirection

The trick question

Read carefully. What does leaving this box unchecked actually do?

signup.example.com
Reveal · How the trick works

The double negative is the whole point.

“Not to not receive” = to receive. The confusing grammar plus mention of “partners” is engineered so that whatever you do, you probably end up opted into marketing. It exploits cognitive load — nobody parses a checkbox twice.

Harm: consent you didn’t knowingly give, data shared with third parties.

Honest version: “Email me occasional updates” — plain, positive, unchecked by default.
