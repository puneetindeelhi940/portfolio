01 — Field Scan

What the ground actually looks like

Four shifts that moved between 2024 and now. Every concept below is aimed at one of them.

8.5s

Auth got solved. Meaning didn't.

Passkey sign-in averages 8.5s against 31.2s for passwords, at ~99% phishing resistance. The hard problem moved: it is no longer entering a credential, it is understanding what you just approved. Recovery is where most rollouts still fail.

FIDO · MSFT Digital Defense
1–5%

The SOC is underwater

Analysts face 100,000+ daily alerts of which 1–5% are true positives; 40–62% are never opened at all. AI triage cuts false positives 60–80%. The real design move: stop treating the alert as the unit of work.

Intezer · arXiv 2605.08316
RFC—draft

Agent consent is unwritten

An IETF draft exists for OAuth on-behalf-of-user because OAuth's assumptions break: it presumes synchronous consent and single-hop delegation. Agents act asynchronously and chain calls across services. No one has designed this surface well yet.

IETF draft-oauth-ai-agents
11%

Fraud targets the person, live

Deepfakes are 11% of global fraud. Banks now detect an active call during a high-risk transfer and intervene mid-flow with pauses and prompts. Timing has become the design material.

Blast Radius

Case queue + downstream blast radius

The problem worth solving

Alert fatigue is not a volume problem, it is a unit-of-work problem. Every console on the market hands the analyst a list of rows and calls it triage. High-performing SOCs have already moved to correlated cases — but almost nobody has designed the interface for that shift.

Layer two: AI now produces the verdict. Which means the analyst's job quietly became adjudicating a machine's confidence under time pressure — and there is no established design language for that at all.

What I'd build

  1. Case, not alert. Related signals collapse into one investigable story with a timeline, so the queue shrinks by an order of magnitude before a human touches it.
  2. The dissent panel. Every AI verdict ships with the argument against itself: "82% benign — here is the 18% case, and here is what would change my mind."
  3. Blast radius map. Before you dismiss, you see what is downstream if you are wrong. Consequence made visible at the moment of decision.
  4. One-key override that teaches. The analyst's call visibly feeds back into the queue — disagreement becomes training data, not a dead end.
Hiring signal

"This person can design for experts operating under load, where a wrong click has consequences." The rarest signal in the UX market.

Scope · blast radius · time · reversibility

The problem worth solving

Today's agent consent screen is a checkbox list inherited from 2012 OAuth. It answers "what APIs" and not one of the four questions a person actually has: how far, how long, how bad if wrong, and how do I stop it?

The standards bodies are openly writing that this is a gap — agents act asynchronously, chain calls across services, and sub-delegate to other agents, all of which OAuth's consent model was never built for. This is the rare case where a designer can arrive before the pattern exists rather than after.

What I'd build

  1. Consent as four dimensions. Scope, blast radius, duration, reversibility — "read your inbox for 7 days, send nothing, spend nothing, revocable in one tap."
  2. The dry run. Before granting, watch a simulation of what this agent would actually do with these permissions. Consent you can see rather than consent you infer.
  3. Standing rules, not interrupts. Set escalation thresholds once ("ask me above $200") instead of being poked forty times a day. Consent fatigue is the real failure mode.
  4. The receipt trail. "What did it do while I was asleep?" gets an answer — a readable ledger with one-tap undo on anything reversible.
  5. Sub-delegation view. When your agent hires another agent, you can see the chain and cut it anywhere along the line.
Hiring signal

"This person is ahead of the market, not competent within it." And it is the exact inverse of the dark-patterns work already in the portfolio — you catalogued manipulative consent, now you design the honest version.

The Pause

Friction as a feature

The problem worth solving

Authorised push payment fraud is the one attack where every control passes. Correct credentials, correct device, correct biometrics — and a person who has been emotionally captured by someone on a phone call. The bank's signals already know: active call, new payee, unusual amount, 2am.

So the entire design surface is fifteen seconds. And the hardest constraint is not the scam victim — it is the ninety-nine people who are not being scammed and just want to send money. False-positive UX is where these products die.

What I'd build

  1. Graduated interruption. Risk signals map to escalating friction — from nothing, to a line of copy, to a hard cooling pause. Calibration is the whole craft.
  2. Copy for a captured mind. Written for someone who is not reading carefully, because they are being talked at. Short, direct, and it names the manipulation out loud.
  3. Name a witness. The single most effective intervention in the research: break the isolation the scammer engineered by asking who else knows about this transfer.
  4. The graceful false positive. The legitimate user gets through fast and does not feel accused — the make-or-break screen nobody puts in a portfolio.
  5. The after-report. What the bank saw and why it stepped in, so trust survives the interruption.
Hiring signal

"This person designs for human beings at their most vulnerable, and still respects the happy path." Demos in 90 seconds and the room goes quiet. Directly on-target for JPMC.

Recommendation

Build The Leash. It is the only one where you arrive early rather than merely good.

Given the two audiences you named, Option 02 is the single strongest bet — it is the one concept that lands hard with AI platform companies and is directly relevant to a bank standing up agentic systems with real money attached. It is also unfalsifiable in the best sense: there is no incumbent product a reviewer can hold it up against, so the conversation becomes about your thinking rather than your pixel parity.

If you want a second piece later, pair it with The Pause — lightest to build, highest emotional payload, and squarely JPMC's problem. Together they read as a designer who can work at both the infrastructure layer and the human layer, which is exactly the staff-level story.

The one I would not lead with: Blast Radius. It is excellent work, but security practitioners will interrogate your domain depth, and that is a fight to pick on your second piece, not your first.

04 — What You Get

Both halves, because a demo alone doesn't get hired

The prototype proves you can make it. The case study proves you knew why.

Part A

The interactive demo

A live, clickable prototype in the portfolio — not a video, not a static mock. Reviewers can push on it themselves.

  • Real interaction states and edge cases
  • A scripted "money shot" moment for the 90-second walkthrough
  • Works on a phone, because that is where it will actually be opened
Part B

The case study

Senior/staff narrative structure — strategic framing over process documentation.

  • The bet: what you believed that others didn't
  • Constraints, trade-offs, and what you deliberately cut
  • The false-positive / failure case, argued honestly
  • What you'd measure and what would prove you wrong
Part C

The interview kit

So the piece survives contact with a hiring panel.

  • A 90-second and a 10-minute version of the story
  • Prepared answers for the three hardest questions
  • Optional: a PPTX cut for portfolio reviews

To start, I need four things from you

  1. Which option — or tell me to build two, in which case I'd sequence Leash first, Pause second.
  2. Real or fictional framing. Is this positioned as speculative concept work, or dressed as a project for an unnamed platform/bank? Changes the tone of every line of copy.
  3. How much of your own history goes in. Any adjacent real work from Dell, Boeing, or elsewhere I can honestly anchor the narrative to — it makes a concept piece land far harder.
  4. Deadline. If there is a specific application in flight, say so and I'll scope to fit it.
