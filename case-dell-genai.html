§ 01 · EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The brief, in one breath.

// what we set out to do and why it mattered

Dell.com is the storefront for one of the largest enterprise hardware businesses on the planet. By 2022, the discovery and configuration experience was structurally sound but cognitively heavy — multi-step configurators, dense compare tables, and a recommendation surface that treated B2B SMB buyers, large-enterprise procurement leads, and self-serve consumers as the same human.

I was asked to lead the pan-Dell GenAI personalization initiative for the .com — a programme that would put generative AI, intent estimation, and behavioural-science nudges into the experience without losing the configurator’s power-user credibility. Four experiences. Thirty-three designers. Five time zones. One coherent practice.

Don’t replace the configurator with a chatbot. Surround the configurator with a colleague who already knows what the buyer is trying to do.

The work shipped across multiple Dell.com verticals over three years. It earned the Dell President’s Award for the GenAI chatbot effort and the Dell Game Changer Award for the broader personalization programme. The patterns we built — prompt libraries, intent rubrics, multi-modal adaptive layouts — are now part of how Dell.com thinks about commerce design.

§ 02 · CONTEXT & COMPLEXITY

Three buyers in one URL.

// enterprise constraints, organisational dynamics, ecosystem

Three things made this work hard. First, three buyers shared one URL: a large-enterprise procurement lead negotiating multi-year contracts; an SMB owner buying a single workstation; and a self-serve consumer cross-shopping XPS against alternatives. Each had different mental models, different tolerance for ambiguity, and different definitions of “a good recommendation.”

Second, the configurator was sacred. For power buyers, the depth of the configurator was the trust signal — it was how they verified Dell understood their use case. Removing it (or hiding it behind a chatbot) was a non-starter. The brief was not “simplify” — it was “wrap.”

Third, the design org was distributed across five time zones. Personalization at Scale wasn’t a single team — it was four parallel PoDs spanning AI, ecommerce, CSAT, and a future-state Sales platform. Each PoD had its own PM, its own engineering SMEs, and its own vertical-specific business sponsors. Coherence was not a free lunch.

I joined the initiative as the design strategist for personalization. My job became twofold: set the design direction for what GenAI on .com should and should not do, and build the operating system that let four PoDs ship against that direction without colliding.

§ 03 · STRATEGIC THINKING

Four decision frameworks.

// the lenses we made the team carry

Before we drew screens, we drew the decision-making system. Four frameworks ended up doing most of the heavy lifting.

  • The Three-Buyer Lens. Every experience review asked, explicitly, how this surface served the procurement lead, the SMB owner, and the self-serve consumer. Trade-offs that hid behind “the user” got named.
  • Configurator-as-Source-of-Truth. GenAI never authored the spec — it authored the path to the spec. The configurator stayed the canonical surface for what you were actually buying. This kept legal, eng, and power buyers all aligned.
  • Intent before recommendation. The personalization stack estimated intent first (what problem are you here to solve?) and only then recommended. This let us tell the difference between “upgrade my home PC” and “refresh my 200-seat fleet” without a quiz.
  • Nudges, not steers. Behavioural-science treatments were applied as gentle nudges (default selection, social proof, recency cues) — not steers (modal interrupts, forced paths). Sales partnered with us to set the line.

Those four frameworks were what 33 designers carried into every PoD ritual. They became a shared language — a way to disagree productively when vertical-specific needs collided with platform-wide consistency.

§ 04 · DESIGN PROCESS

How the work moved.

// research → architecture → rituals → ship

We ran the programme as a five-stage loop. Each stage had named artefacts, named owners, and a named exit criterion — there were no “wait, what stage are we in” conversations.

01 · LISTEN
B2B + B2C research
Extensive interviews across vertical and geography. Built strong use cases and behavioural patterns for new market opportunities.
02 · MAP
E2E journeys
End-to-end customer journeys for the future-state Sales & CSAT platform — the connective tissue between PoDs.
03 · PROMPT
Prompt libraries
Versioned prompt libraries with intent rubrics and evaluation harnesses. Treated prompts as design artefacts.
04 · ADAPT
Multi-modal layouts
Adaptive layouts that responded to estimated intent and surface (chat, configurator, compare). One system, many faces.
05 · SHIP
Vertical-specific roll-outs
Frictionless, vertical-specific solutions — partnered with Sales & verticals to land each market on its own terms.

Two rituals deserve calling out. The 3-6-9 month deliverable plan — every PoD’s next three quarters were on one wall, refreshed monthly, visible to every designer. And the cross-PoD design crit — a single weekly review where the four leads brought one piece of work each, scored against the four frameworks. That crit is where most of the “wait, why are we doing it this way” conversations actually happened.

§ 05 · LEADERSHIP LAYER

The 33-designer practice.

// influence, mentorship, conflict, coherence

Thirty-three designers across five time zones is a coordination problem before it is a design problem. The people work was the work. A few of the things that mattered most:

  • Mentorship by rotation. Every designer had a six-month mentorship arc covering People, Process, and Technology. Senior designers rotated through. The investment up front made disagreements later much cheaper — we were already a team before we needed to be one.
  • Negotiation & storytelling coaching. I coached designers explicitly on stakeholder communication and storytelling. Most design work fails not in the file but in the meeting; we treated the meeting as part of the craft.
  • Executive-level presence. Represented the team in executive presentations and strategic discussions. I treated this as an extension of mentoring — taking senior designers into rooms they wouldn’t otherwise have been in.
  • Asynchronous discipline. Five time zones means most decisions had to land in writing. I invested heavily in async written rationales — memos, decision logs, recorded crits — so the team in EMEA wasn’t a second-class participant.
A 33-person design org doesn’t scale by adding designers. It scales by adding rituals that designers can run without you in the room.

What changed.

The programme shipped across multiple Dell.com verticals over three years. Some of the most legible outcomes:

§ 07 · REFLECTION

What I’d do differently.

// the honest postmortem

Two things, with the benefit of hindsight.

One — I’d invest in evaluation tooling earlier. We had prompt libraries before we had a great way to evaluate prompt drift across verticals. The first six months of evaluation was “screenshots in Slack” — fine for the first sprint, expensive across thirty-three designers. If I were starting again, the eval harness would be a Q1 deliverable, not a Q4 retrofit.

Two — I’d codify the “configurator-as-source-of-truth” principle sooner. It was implicit in our heads for a year before we wrote it down. Every week we paid an unnecessary tax in the form of a debate that had already been settled, we just hadn’t written down where. The lesson generalises: codify the implicit rules before they cost you twenty meetings.

A third thing, which is less a regret and more a forward-look: the next version of this work isn’t a chatbot at all — it’s the storefront treating the AI agent as a buying colleague the customer brings with them. That’s the work I want to do next.
