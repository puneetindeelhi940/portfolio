My Personalization Journey

Nineteen years of trying to make software pay attention — and slowly learning that the hard part was never the algorithm.

~6 min read·A first-person note

I’ve spent a large part of my career on personalization — including three years running Personalization at Scale. This is the short version of how my thinking changed: from chasing relevance, to distrusting it, to rebuilding it around trust.

01

The promise I fell for

I started where most of us start: convinced that if a product could just know enough about a person, it would finally feel effortless. The right product, the right message, the right moment. It read like a design ideal and a business slam-dunk at once, and I chased it hard.

For a while the wins were real and intoxicating. Reorder the shelf by what someone seemed to want, and the numbers moved. Swap the hero to match the campaign, and they moved again. It was easy to conclude that more data plus more model equalled more relevance — and that relevance was the whole game.

02

The disillusionment

Two things wore the shine off. The first was how often ‘AI personalization’ turned out to be a stack of hand-written rules wearing a nicer name. Teams would call segments ‘one-to-one’ and call if-statements ‘intelligence’, and the roadmap would argue past itself because personalization meant five different things to five people in the same meeting.

The second was quieter and harder. The more precisely we targeted, the more some of it started to feel like something done to people rather than for them — urgency that wasn’t real, memory nobody asked for, a funnel optimised at the user’s expense. The metrics were green and something still felt off.

The model wasn’t the bottleneck. Trust was.

That was the turn. I stopped asking ‘how do we predict this person better?’ and started asking ‘would this person be glad we did that, if they could see it?’

03

The reframe

Personalization, I decided, is a trust transaction before it is a revenue one. Every adaptive move spends a little of a person’s trust and, done well, pays it back with interest. Done badly, it quietly overdraws the account — and you only find out when they leave.

That reframe changed the craft in concrete ways. Show the reason for every guess, so a person can see and correct it. Make forgetting as easy as remembering. Infer from what someone did, never from sensitive things about who they are. And judge the whole system by lift against an honest holdout, not by the revenue it conveniently claims credit for.

04

What I build now

These days the journey lives in artefacts rather than slides. I built a maturity model so a team can locate itself honestly instead of flattering itself; a personalization engine to prototype adaptive experiences that stay legible; and a running argument, across everything in this lab, that trust is the real moat — not the model.

I’m not done. Personalization keeps getting more capable and therefore more able to overreach, and the discipline of designing it in someone’s favour only gets more important. But the through-line is settled: the goal was never to know the person. It was to earn the right to act like it.
